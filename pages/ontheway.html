The Problem & Research:

PROXIMITY ≠ CONVENIENCE

proximity doesn’t necessarily mean convenience
When people travel and want to stop for food, map tools rank by distance from a point. But a place can be 0.3 miles away and still cost 15 minutes off the route.
Finding food shouldn’t mean going out of your way.
The closest place to you isn’t always the closest to your trip.

TRADEOFFS I MADE

Ranking

Because detour time decides where people stop, results rank by distance from the route first. I kept ratings as a secondary signal. Cut: sorting by popularity, which kept surfacing top-rated places far off the path.

Coverage

Splitting the route into thirds and picking from each balances coverage against API cost and latency. More sample points found few extra places but added a request per point, which slowed results noticeably.

Filtering

Showing everything recreated the “nearby” problem. Hiding options more than 0.5 km from the route keeps the list decision-ready.

Scope

No accounts, reservations, or multi-stop trips yet. Goal was to validate the core value: people choose where to eat by how little it adds to their trip, not by how close it is to them.

Reflection:

WHAT I LEARNED & WHAT’S NEXT

Learned

Location data is messier than it looks. Matching boroughs by address text broke on inconsistent formats, so I switched to polygon boundaries, which made the NYC filter reliable.

I’d measure

Minutes added per stop, compared with the top “nearby” result on the same route. If OnTheWay’s picks don’t cost less time, the idea doesn’t hold.

Next

Multi-stop trips and cities beyond NYC. Accounts and reservations come after the core ranking is proven.
