In a city that never stops eating, sustainability is rarely on the menu.

The problem

Sustainability shouldn't be a research project.

NYU students pick where to eat by convenience, price, taste and proximity. Many want to choose more sustainably, but a restaurant's environmental impact is never visible at the moment they decide.

The data exists, but it's scattered across city datasets. So sustainability ends up an afterthought.

Research

What makes a restaurant "sustainable"?

Instead of treating sustainability as one number, I combined three public NYC datasets, building energy and water use (LL84) and health inspections (DOHMH), into one Green Score per restaurant.

What I found
01

Raw data doesn't help

Energy and water numbers mean little to someone deciding where to grab dinner.

02

Context matters

Location, neighborhood and nearby alternatives all shape an everyday food decision.

03

The meal matters too

Even at a greener restaurant, different dishes can have very different impacts.

The solution

Build sustainability into discovery.

Where

Discover

Browse NYC neighborhoods on a map and see the greenest restaurants in each.

Which

Compare

Compare restaurants with one Green Score instead of reading raw datasets.

What

Understand

Scan a menu to estimate the carbon footprint of individual dishes.

How it works, step by step
  1. Load the neighborhood map

    Mapbox GL renders 10 clickable NYC neighborhood polygons across Manhattan and Brooklyn, with hover states.

    NeighborhoodMap.jsx · Mapbox GL 3
  2. Seed restaurant + building data

    DOHMH inspection records and LL84 building energy records are fetched and matched to each restaurant by address.

    services/nyc_open_data.py · scripts/seed.py
  3. Score energy, water, health

    Each restaurant is scored on energy efficiency (50 pts), water efficiency (25 pts) and health grade (25 pts).

    services/scoring.py
  4. Calculate the Green Score

    The three parts add up to one 0–100 score, tiered green (70+), yellow (50–69) or red (under 50).

    services/scoring.py · green_score()
  5. Rank top 5 per neighborhood

    The sidebar surfaces the greenest restaurants in each area, filterable between Restaurants and Sips & Sweets.

    routers/restaurants.py
  6. Search or browse

    Search thousands of restaurants live, or click a map pin. Both show the score badge and neighborhood.

    SearchBar.jsx
  7. View the score breakdown

    The detail view splits the score across energy, water and health with progress bars.

    MapPage.jsx
  8. Scan a menu (optional)

    Upload a photo of a menu. OpenAI Vision reads each dish and estimates its carbon footprint.

    menu_scanner.py
  9. Bookmark & log orders

    Save restaurants and log what you ordered to build a personal history of choices.

    UserProfile.jsx · AuthContext.jsx
  10. Grow the plant avatar

    Logged orders add up to a CO₂ savings total, and the plant avatar grows through 5 stages as it rises.

    WateringAvatar.jsx

Built with React, FastAPI, Mapbox GL, NYC Open Data (LL84, DOHMH) and OpenAI Vision. Deployed on Vercel.

Tradeoffs

What I chose, and what I cut.

One score, details on demand

A single 0–100 score lets people compare at a glance. The energy, water and health split lives one tap deeper in the detail view, for anyone who wants to check the math.

Missing data

Restaurants without EnergyStar data get a neutral 25 of 50 instead of being dropped. Small spots stay on the map, at the cost of a less precise score.

A shortlist, not a directory

The sidebar shows only the top 5 greenest places per neighborhood. Search still covers thousands of restaurants, but browsing stays decision-ready.

Scope

Launched with 10 neighborhoods across Manhattan and Brooklyn, where NYU's campuses are, instead of all of NYC. The goal was to validate the core value: people will pick a greener option when the impact is visible.

Testing

Audience: NYU students

"I had no idea this data existed, cool how you can quantify eco-friendliness."

Tester, NYU junior living near Washington Square

What I changed: testers didn't fully trust the score until they saw where it came from, so I added the energy, water and health breakdown to the detail view.

Reflection

What I learned

The data was never the product. City energy and water records only became useful once they turned into one score people could read in a second.

What I'd measure

Share of logged orders from green-tier restaurants over time. If it doesn't rise, the score informs people without changing what they do.

What's next

More neighborhoods beyond the launch 10, and showing that menu carbon numbers are AI estimates so users know how much to trust them.
